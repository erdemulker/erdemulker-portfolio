Case 03

Promotions, lifecycle and operations

Arguing for the project nobody had funded

Promotions had been built quickly as a small feature: a display name, active or inactive, a start and an end date. They were also responsible for roughly a tenth of platform revenue, and account managers were tracking hundreds of them in side spreadsheets because the product could not tell them what state anything was in.

The decision that mattered

Five explicit states: scheduled, active, expired, draft and inactive.

What it changed

+12% publisher created promotions created by publishers with self serve access, over a two month period after release.

Five explicit states in the table, and an expired promotion one click from reactivation.

Context

We earn on a cost per install basis from the offers shown inside Playtime, and promotions are one of the strongest levers on that. They had been built early, kept small on purpose, and never revisited: a name, a binary status, two dates. Account managers ran hundreds of them through that interface, using external trackers to keep hold of what was live, what was queued and what had lapsed. The first thing I did was unrelated to the lifecycle. I linked each promotion to the publisher using it, because the table gave no context about who a promotion belonged to.

Before. Two statuses, no filtering, no date range. Everything a person needed to know about timing had to be inferred from the name or tracked somewhere else.

Tension

Promotions sat in the backlog behind several much larger initiatives, and the internal view, held in good faith, was that they worked. Nobody disputed that the table was crude. They disputed that it mattered. So I made the cost of leaving it alone visible. I put a number on the revenue promotions carried, roughly ten percent of the platform, which everyone had sensed and nobody had written down. Then I documented the hours account managers spent reconciling the table against their own trackers, and the risk of a promotion running past its window, or never starting, because nobody noticed. Presented together, recurring cost against unrealised revenue, it got prioritised. Getting there took longer than the design did.

Insight

Promotions already had a lifecycle. It ran in production every day; the product just had no words for it. A promotion is scheduled, goes active, expires, and if it recurs goes back to scheduled. Alongside that it can sit half configured as a draft, or be parked as inactive. All of that was being flattened into one flag, so account managers rebuilt the missing states in spreadsheets. I did not have to invent a model. I had to write down the one they were already using.

The spreadsheets were the specification. Account managers had written the lifecycle down; the product had not.

Decisions, and what each one cost

Infer state from dates, or model it explicitly
What I choseFive explicit states: scheduled, active, expired, draft and inactive.
What it costDeriving status from start and end dates would have been cheaper and needed no data changes. It also cannot work: a recurring promotion between runs and a deliberately parked one both look identical to a date comparison, and a half filled form has no dates at all. The three states that caused the confusion are the three that cannot be inferred.
Keep the flow linear, or add confirmation steps
What I choseTwo interruptions: a confirmation before editing or deactivating a promotion that is already running, and a lightweight modal for reactivating an expired one.
What it costThe PM argued against both, reasonably, on the grounds that we had never had confirmation dialogs and they slow a linear process down. I disagreed, because these actions are asymmetric. Changing a live promotion changes what end users see immediately and cannot be undone by pressing back, and we had already paid for one mistake of exactly this kind. A second of friction is cheaper. We shipped them.
Where the date range control belongs
What I choseIn the page top bar, beside filter, search and the primary action.
What it costMy first pass put the date picker inside the filter panel, which was faster to build and locally sensible. An internal review pointed out we already have an established top bar pattern hosting filter, search, date range and CTA together. I had introduced an inconsistency without noticing. Moving it cost a revision and kept the pattern intact.
Better search, or structured filtering
What I choseA filter system across status, publisher, application and active date, plus sortable columns.
What it costSearch alone only works if the title carries every attribute someone might look for, so titles had turned into improvised metadata. Filtering moves that information into structure, at the cost of a heavier top bar.

Leadership and ownership

  • Making an invisible cost visibleNobody was arguing that promotions worked well. They were arguing that other things mattered more. I quantified the revenue promotions carried and documented the operational time account managers were losing to external trackers, then presented both as an ongoing cost rather than a feature request.
  • Disagreeing with my PM, and saying whyThe PM wanted no confirmation dialogs, for consistency with everything else in the product. I pushed for two, argued from a specific class of past incident and the asymmetry of the actions, and shipped them. Being consistent was the weaker argument here.
  • Accepting a correction in reviewThe date picker was in the wrong place and an internal review caught it. It was my inconsistency to fix, and I would rather absorb a revision than weaken a pattern the rest of the product depends on.

What shipped

Five lifecycle states made explicit in the table, with filtering, search and a date range control in the top bar. Previously this was a binary flag and a search box.

The lifecycle as it already ran in production. The loop is the part a date comparison can express. Draft and inactive are the part it cannot, and the part account managers had been tracking by hand.

Measured over two months on a base of several hundred promotions. The gap between the two numbers is the part worth reading. Account managers gained more than publishers, which fits: they run the volume, and they were the ones keeping spreadsheets. The publishers with access are a small self serve group and lean on promotions less than we do.

More: the system underneath, step by step and more screens

The system underneath

Five states, with the transitions that actually occur. The happy path is a loop rather than a line, which is the detail the original two state model could not express.

Scheduled
  • Created, waiting
  • Returns here if recurring
Active
  • Running now
  • Changing it asks first
Expired
  • Window closed
  • Reactivate via modal
Draft
  • Form half filled
  • 10 to 20 inputs, saved partway
Inactive
  • Parked deliberately
  • Not derivable from dates

Scheduled, active and expired form the recurring loop. Draft and inactive sit outside it, and are the two states no date based logic could ever produce. That is why they had been invisible.

How it works, step by step

  1. See state at a glanceEvery promotion carries its actual lifecycle state in the table, so the question of what is live right now stops requiring a side app.
  2. Filter instead of guessStatus, publisher, application and active date, with sortable columns, so finding a set of promotions no longer depends on how someone titled them.
  3. Set a date rangeA range control in the top bar for checking everything active or planned within a window, which is how account managers actually think about their month.
  4. Change a running promotion, deliberatelyEditing or deactivating something that is live asks for confirmation and states the consequence, because end users are already seeing it.
  5. Reactivate without rebuildingAn expired promotion can be brought back through a short modal rather than the full creation form, since the configuration is already correct.

The model, and the two places it needed friction

Friction added on purpose. Editing or deactivating a running promotion changes what end users see immediately, so both state the consequence before they proceed.

Friction removed on purpose. An expired promotion returns by setting a new window rather than reopening the full creation form, because the rest of its configuration is already correct.

Five dimensions to filter, and the date range rule. A promotion is returned if it touches the window at any point, because account managers think in windows rather than in whole promotions.

Looking back

This is the case I would use to explain what changes at senior level. A mid level designer could have drawn these screens. The work was showing that a feature everyone considered finished was costing us revenue and hours every week, and turning that into funded engineering time. The table is the smaller half of what shipped.
