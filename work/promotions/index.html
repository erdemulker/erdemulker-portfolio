Case 03

Promotions, lifecycle and operations

Arguing for the project nobody had funded

Promotions shipped early as a small feature: a name, active or inactive, two dates. They also carried about 10% of platform revenue, and account managers tracked hundreds of them in side spreadsheets because the product could not say what state anything was in.

RoleProduct designer, end to end
TeamPM, engineering, account managers as primary users
TimelineAnalysis, then design in stages
Year2026
The decision that mattered

Five explicit states: scheduled, active, expired, draft and inactive.

What it changed

+12% publisher created promotions created by self serve publishers, two months after release.

Status is a filter like any other. An expired promotion is one filter and one click from reactivation.

Context

We earn per install on the offers inside Playtime, and promotions are one of the strongest levers on that. My first change had nothing to do with lifecycle: I linked each promotion to its publisher, because the table did not say whose it was.

Before. Two statuses, no filters, no date range. Timing had to be read from the name or tracked somewhere else.

Tension

Promotions sat behind much larger projects, and the view was that they worked. Nobody disputed the table was crude. They disputed that it mattered. So I put numbers on it: roughly 10% of platform revenue, the hours AMs spent reconciling side trackers, and the risk of a promotion overrunning or never starting. Framed as a recurring cost, it got funded. That took longer than the design.

Insight

The lifecycle already ran in production every day: scheduled, active, expired, and back to scheduled when recurring, plus drafts and deliberately parked promotions. One flag flattened all of it, so AMs rebuilt the states in spreadsheets. I wrote down the model they were already using.

The spreadsheets were the spec.

Decisions, and what each one cost

Infer state from dates, or model it
What I choseFive explicit states: scheduled, active, expired, draft and inactive.
What it costDates would have been cheaper. They cannot tell a recurring promotion between runs from a parked one, and a draft has no dates. The three states causing confusion are exactly the three dates cannot express.
Keep the flow linear, or add friction
What I choseA confirmation before editing or deactivating a live promotion, and a short modal to reactivate an expired one.
What it costMy PM argued against both, since we had no confirmation dialogs anywhere. These actions are asymmetric, and we had already paid for one live edit mistake. We shipped them.
Better search, or structured filters
What I choseFilters for status, publisher, application, targeting and date, plus sortable columns.
What it costSearch only works if titles carry every attribute, so titles had become improvised metadata. Filters move that into structure, at the cost of a busier top bar.

Leadership and ownership

  • Making an invisible cost visibleNobody said promotions worked well. They said other things mattered more. I quantified the revenue and the AM hours and presented them as a recurring cost.
  • Disagreeing with my PMI argued for two confirmations from a past incident and the asymmetry of the actions. Consistency was the weaker argument here.
  • Taking a review correctionI first put the date picker inside the filter panel, which broke our established top bar pattern. A review caught it and I moved it.

What shipped

Friction on purpose. Editing or deactivating a live promotion says who it reaches before it goes through.

Friction removed on purpose. An expired promotion comes back by setting a new window, without rebuilding the form.

Impact

+12% publisher createdpromotions created by self serve publishers, two months after release
+18% AM createdpromotions created by account managers, same window, on several hundred active

Account managers gained more than publishers. That fits: they run the volume, and they were the ones keeping spreadsheets.

More: the system underneath and more screens

The system underneath

Five states, with the transitions that actually happen.

Scheduled
  • Waiting for its window
  • Returns here if recurring
Active
  • Running now
  • Edits ask first
Expired
  • Window closed
  • Reactivate via modal
Draft
  • Half filled
  • Saved partway
Inactive
  • Parked on purpose
  • Not derivable from dates

Scheduled, active and expired form the loop. Draft and inactive sit outside it, which is why no date logic could ever produce them.

The model behind it

A date range returns any promotion that touches the window, because AMs plan in windows.

One publisher, one promotion in each state.

The loop is what a date comparison can express. Draft and inactive are what it cannot, and what AMs had been tracking by hand.

Looking back

The screens were the easy half. The work was showing that a feature everyone called finished was costing revenue and hours every week, and turning that into funded engineering time.
